- This is **not legal advice.** It's general information that explains official guidance and service terms in plain language. For real disputes or big contracts, talk to a professional.
- Korea's official guidance (as of June 2025) holds that **AI output with no creative contribution from a person can't be registered for copyright.** It also explains that entering a prompt alone is hard to recognize as a creative contribution.
- On the other hand, if AI output **resembles someone else's work, it can become a problem**, and the guidance says responsibility usually lies with the person who generated and used the output.
- Making something just for yourself and **posting it publicly or using it to make money** carry different levels of risk. For the latter, always check before posting.
- Each service has different terms. **Read the official terms** of the service you use yourself.

## What you'll be able to do after this tutorial

- Get the big picture of whether AI-made output has copyright, and when it's hard to recognize.

## Concepts to know first

| Term | Plain meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Work (copyrighted work)** | A creation that expresses a person's thoughts or feelings. It doesn't have to be grand: a photo taken by an amateur is a work if it has creativity. That's an example from the guide. |
| **Author** | The person who actually made the work. The guide explains that someone who only came up with an idea, or paid for it to be made, is hard to regard as the author. |
| **AI output** | A result that the AI produced as instructed by a person, but with **no creative contribution from the person**. This is the guide's term. |
| **AI-assisted work** | A work a person made **using AI as a tool**, with some part where the person's creative contribution is recognized. This is the guide's term. |
| **Copyright infringement** | Acts such as copying or altering someone else's work without permission. The guide holds that the existing standards (whether it was made by looking at someone else's work, and whether the expression is substantially similar) apply to AI output in the same way. |
| **Portrait rights and personality rights** | Rights protected separately from copyright, such as those over your face, voice, and reputation. |

### How do AI output and AI-assisted work differ?

Put simply, here's how the guide draws the line.

- A picture, text, or music you made by entering only a prompt and using the result as is is likely **AI output**. The guide says it isn't eligible for copyright registration.
- Parts where you supplied your own rough sketch, or selected, arranged, and edited the AI results to add your own expression, are **parts where a person's contribution may be recognized**. The guide says registration is possible for those parts only.
- Even if registered, the effect extends **only to the part the person contributed**.
- The guide clearly states that the final decision on whether there is human contribution is made by the court.

<Aside type="note" title="How is the prompt itself treated?">
The guide explains that entering a prompt is generally evaluated as giving an idea or an instruction. The reason is that results differ each time even with the same prompt, so it's hard for a person to control or predict them. The guide also notes that countries take different positions on this question. It also explains that a long, creative prompt text can be a work in its own right, but that doesn't mean the output produced from that prompt is recognized as creative.
</Aside>

<Aside type="caution" title="What this tutorial couldn't confirm">

<Steps>

1. **Decide the use first: just for me, to post, or to sell**

   The guide explains that an individual making AI output might not be infringement, but **publishing or sharing it externally** can cause problems. It advises extra care when there's a profit motive, such as advertising, publishing, or merchandising. So deciding the use first gives you a sense of how careful to be.

2. **Don't put other people's work directly into the prompt**

   The guide advises avoiding entering the exact text of a particular novel or song lyrics, or phrasing that steers the AI toward identical or similar output. It gives examples like these as potentially problematic:

   - "Make an illustration featuring (a specific company)'s (specific character)"
   - "Show me the whole novel by (a specific author) word for word"
   - "Make a new episode of (a specific comic) in the original art style"
   - "Make a song in the voice of (a specific singer)," "Make a T-shirt with the (brand) logo" (these are rights issues other than copyright)

   The guide adds that entering such a prompt isn't in itself an infringement; it can become a problem when the result is the same as or similar to existing work. It also warns that even if you included part of someone's text just for reference, important parts of it can end up in the result.

   <PromptBox>
   I want to make an image to introduce my brand. Don't imitate any particular artist or character. Suggest 3 directions for a warm-toned neighborhood bakery illustration. Please give the explanations in English.
   </PromptBox>

3. **Check whether the result resembles someone else's**

   The guide explains that responsibility for infringement generally lies with the **user** who entered the prompt to generate the output, and that the service provider can also be responsible in certain cases. It also explains that the risk can be high if the user knew of a particular work and entered specific prompts several times so the result would resemble its expression. Conversely, it says that when an ordinary request like "a picture of a cute cat" happens to produce something similar by chance, it doesn't immediately treat that as infringement. Where infringement begins differs case by case, so this tutorial can't draw that line for you.

   In practice, try this:

   - For output you'll post externally, use **image search** (Google Lens, Naver image search for users in Korea, and so on) to see if similar existing work exists.
   - For text, search eye-catching sentences in quotation marks and see if the original turns up.
   - If something recognizable is included, such as a logo, a character, or a famous person's face, it's safer not to use it.

4. **Be careful with faces, voices, and trademarks, separately from copyright**

   The guide notes that besides copyright, rights such as personality rights, portrait rights, and trade secrets can be infringed. Altering a real person's photo with AI, imitating a voice, or putting company secrets into a prompt each need separate care. We covered the problem of entering confidential information in [tutorial 5: Privacy and safety](/basics/05-privacy-safety/).

5. **Read the terms of the service you use yourself**

   The guide says that AI services state in their terms who owns the output's copyright and whether commercial use is allowed, so be sure to check and don't go beyond what's permitted. Here is what this tutorial confirmed directly on the official terms pages.

   | Service | What we confirmed | Date and version |

   | **Claude** (Consumer Terms) | As long as you comply with the terms, Anthropic assigns to you whatever rights it has in the Outputs, if any ("if any"). You keep your rights in your inputs. It also notes that outputs may contain inaccuracies, so don't rely on them without checking. | Version effective 2025-10-08, checked 2026-10-14 |
   | **Gemini** (Google Terms of Service) | It states that Google doesn't claim ownership of content you create with the services. It also lists, as an example of prohibited conduct, deceiving others by presenting generative AI content as if it were human-made. | Version effective 2026-07-30, checked 2026-10-14 |

7. **Be honest that you used AI**

   How much you need to disclose depends on the rules of your school, company, contest, or publisher. This tutorial doesn't claim a general legal obligation. Two things were confirmed:

   - Google's terms list deceiving others by presenting generative AI content as human-made as an example of prohibited conduct.
   - The Korea Copyright Commission's guide warns that if you falsely apply for copyright registration presenting AI output as if a person created it, you **can be punished for false registration**. It directs applicants to separate the AI-output parts from the human-created parts on the registration form.

   As a habit, it's safest to say plainly something like "I made the draft with AI and edited it myself." For school assignments or contests, read the relevant rules first.

8. **Check that the result is true, too**

   Another pillar of ethics is accuracy. AI can be plausibly wrong. Check numbers, quotes, and sources yourself as in [tutorial 4: How to fact-check](/basics/04-fact-checking/), and before showing it to others, ask once more whether you can stand behind it.

</Steps>

## Quick reference

| Situation | The big picture under the guide | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Using a picture made from a prompt only, as is | Without a person's creative contribution, it isn't eligible for copyright registration. Final decision rests with the court | Don't assume you have exclusive rights |
| Selecting and arranging AI pictures and finishing them by hand | Registration may be possible for the human-contributed parts (effective only for those parts) | Keep a record of what work you did |
| Refining my own rough sketch with AI | If my creative work remains recognizable in the result, registration may be possible | Keep the original sketch |
| Instructing a specific character or artist's style | If it resembles existing work, it can be an infringement issue | Don't instruct it, and if it looks similar, don't use it |
| Using a real person's face or voice | Beyond copyright, portrait and personality rights issues can arise | Don't use without the person's consent |
| Using it only for personal notes | The guide holds that personal generation alone might not be infringement | Still recheck before sharing externally |
| Using it in ads, products, or publishing | Commercial purposes are described as needing more advance checking | Search for similar works + check terms + consult an expert |

## Common sticking points

### "If AI made it, isn't it not my work?"

- **Cause**: "Made by AI" and "I used AI as a tool" get mixed together.
- **Fix**: The guide doesn't treat output with no creative human contribution as a work, but says the parts a person contributed (edits, selection and arrangement, inputs you wrote yourself) can be works. Which side you're on differs case by case, and the court makes the final call.

### "The terms say commercial use is okay. Is that safe?"

- **Cause**: Permission under the terms and infringing a third party's rights are separate matters.
- **Fix**: The terms are an agreement between the service and you. They don't prevent problems that arise because the output resembles someone else's work. Even the Korean service terms cited in the guide include a clause that members are responsible for using the output.

### "I want to stop others from using my writing and art in AI"

- **Cause**: AI training is a worry from the creator's side too.
- **Fix**: The guide (as of June 2025) states that there is not yet a court ruling on whether using works for AI training without permission is infringement, and introduces, for reference, methods such as rights holders stating their refusal explicitly or restricting crawlers with robots.txt. It also notes that there's no established interpretation of their legal effect.

### "A dispute came up. Where do I ask?"

- **Cause**: It's hard for an individual to judge alone.
- **Fix**: For users in Korea, the Korea Copyright Commission offers a desk with lawyers dedicated to AI consultations (call 1800-5455, then press 0, per that page as of 2026-10-14). The guide also introduces a dispute mediation system. For specific cases, talk to a lawyer.

## Practical tips

- Collecting the **prompts, before-and-after files, and work dates** in a folder when you make output makes it easier to explain what contribution you made.
- For jobs that need an entirely new work (a logo, brand imagery), it's safer and better in quality to **treat the AI result as a draft and refine it yourself**.
- If your school or company has an AI usage policy, **that policy takes priority** over this tutorial.
- Guidelines can change. Before anything big, open the Korea Copyright Commission's latest materials once more.
